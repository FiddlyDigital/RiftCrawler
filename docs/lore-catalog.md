# An Chomhaireamh — The Reckoning of the Deep

*Everything the ogham stone in the mound has to say, set down in order.*

---

### How to read this

This is the catalogue as the **seanchaí** would tell it: every named thing in the deep, in his
voice, with no numbers in it. What a thing *does* is described the way a story describes it, not
the way a ledger does.

Beneath many entries you will find a note in a second voice, marked like this:

> **Of the source —** where the figure comes from in the Irish tradition, and where this telling
> departs from it.

That second voice is the scholar's, not the seanchaí's. Skip those lines and you have a lore book.
Read only those lines and you have an audit of how close the game sits to its sources. A short
appendix at the end names the two places where it sits furthest away.

Irish myth survives in more than one recension and the versions disagree with each other freely.
Where the tradition is genuinely divided, the notes say so rather than picking a winner.

---

## I. The War Above, and the Bridge Below

Bres the Beautiful was our king once, and he was beautiful, and he was a disaster.

He came of two houses — a Fomorian father off the sea, a mother of the Tuatha Dé — and when Nuada
lost his sword-arm at the first battle of Mag Tuired and could no longer hold the kingship, it was
Bres who was given it. A blemished king cannot rule; a beautiful one may. So the reasoning went.

He taxed the smoke of our fires. He set the Dagda to digging ditches and Ogma to hauling firewood.
No guest left his hall with grease on his knife or the smell of ale on his breath, and for a people
who measure a king by his table that was the whole of the indictment. Cairbre the poet made the
first satire ever spoken in Ériu against him, and Bres's face broke out in boils, and a king with
boils is no king. He went to his father's people over the sea and came back with the Fomorian host,
and at the second battle of Mag Tuired we broke them.

We did not kill him. That was the mercy, and this deep is what the mercy cost.

He is down here now, building. Every cold hearth in Ériu, every stolen harvest, every thing the
land lost under him — he is quarrying it out of the dark and laying it end to end into a causeway,
and when the causeway touches the shore he will walk back across it at the head of everything that
hates us. The stone falls because he is quarrying. That is all the falling stone has ever been.

You are going down there to break it.

> **Of the source —** Bres, his miserliness, Cairbre's satire, the deposition and the second battle
> of Mag Tuired are all as the tradition has them; the detail about grease on knives and ale on
> breath is close to the wording of *Cath Maige Tuired* itself. In the source he is spared in
> exchange for teaching the Tuatha Dé ploughing and sowing — agriculture, not architecture. **The
> causeway is this telling's invention entirely.** It is a good one: it gives a spared villain
> somewhere to go, and it makes the falling stone mean something.

## II. Those Who Bar the Way

Five things stand between the surface and the bottom, and each of them was old before Bres thought
of his bridge. They are not his. He has simply found them down here and let them be.

### Oilliphéist, the Great Worm
The earth shakes before it arrives, which is the only courtesy it offers. It was cutting channels
through this island long before anything walked on it, and it will be cutting them after.

> **Of the source —** a serpent-monster of Irish folk tradition, credited with gouging out the
> course of the Shannon as it fled westward. Folklore rather than the Mythological Cycle proper.

### Donn, Lord of the Dead
He keeps a house on a rock off the south-west coast and every soul in Ireland comes to it
eventually. *To me you shall all come* — that was the whole of his promise, and he has never once
had to repeat it. To make a road that leads *away* from his house is close to blasphemy.

> **Of the source —** Donn son of Míl, drowned off the coast during the Milesian landing, whose
> dwelling Tech Duinn became the assembly-house of the Irish dead. Strictly he belongs to the
> **Milesian** invasion — the people who displaced the Tuatha Dé — rather than to the Tuatha Dé
> themselves.

### The Dullahan
Hoofbeats and no rider to make them. He calls a name, and whoever owns the name stops owning
anything. There is no bargaining, no riddle, no test. He is simply a summons that happens to have
horses.

> **Of the source —** the headless coachman of later Irish folklore, well outside the Mythological
> Cycle. One of the deep's borrowings from a younger tradition.

### Abhartach
Buried three times and standing up each time, because he was buried standing — which is the mistake
everyone made and nobody learned from. He asks for blood in a bowl and he is patient about it.

> **Of the source —** a chieftain-sorcerer from Derry folklore, killed and reburied repeatedly by
> Cathán, finally held by a burial upside-down under a stone. Sometimes claimed as an ancestor of
> the literary vampire; the claim is thinner than the people making it would like.

### Caoránach
The mother. Every crawling thing in the deep came out of her at some point, and she is not finished.
A thousand eyes, and every one of them counting you.

> **Of the source —** the monstrous mother of demons associated with Lough Derg, driven into the
> lake in the Saint Patrick material. Christian-era folklore, and the furthest of the five from the
> Tuatha Dé.

### And at the end of it, Bres
The causeway finished, and him first across it. He does not descend on you; he descends on the
world, and you are merely standing in the doorway. When he is hurt badly enough he calls for his
own — two of the Fomorian host, out of the dark beside him — because he has never in his life
finished a thing by himself.

## III. The Creatures of the Deep

Small things, mostly. They are not Bres's soldiers so much as what has crept into his quarry.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Bog Rat** | The bottom of the reckoning. Comes at you head-on because it has never thought of anything else. |
| **Cairn Wight** | Something that was buried under a cairn and has stopped respecting the arrangement. What it leaves in a wound goes on working after it is dead. |
| **Fomorian Slinger** | Keeps its distance and makes you close it. One of Bres's, and it fights like a man who expects to be paid. |
| **Peat Ooze** | Barely fights at all. It mends the things beside it, which is far worse — kill the ooze first or kill everything twice. |
| **Fomorian Brute** | Big, and gets worse. Wound it badly and it stops defending itself entirely and simply hits you twice as often. The most dangerous moment of a brute is the moment before it dies. |
| **Sluagh Bat** | Fast, poisonous, and never where it was. It comes out of the host of the restless dead and it moves like something with nothing left to lose. |

> **Of the source —** the **Sluagh** are the host of the unforgiven dead in Irish and Scottish
> tradition, said to come from the west and take the dying with them; the bat-shape is this
> telling's. **Fomorians** are the sea-people the Tuatha Dé fought — in the older strata they are
> neighbours and rivals as much as monsters, which the Seanchaí is right to insist on. A **cairn
> wight** is a fantasy construction on an Irish burial practice, not a figure from the tradition.

## IV. Those Met Wandering

The deep is not empty of people. Some of them are even glad to see you.

### The Seanchaí of the Mound
The keeper of the tale. He has the whole of it — Bres's reign, Lugh at the door of Tara, the three
craft-gods, the sea's memory of the Fomorians as neighbours — and he will give you any piece of it
for the cost of sitting down. He also watches how you descend, and he will tell you plainly whether
you rush the floors, dawdle on them, or judge them about right.

> **Of the source —** *seanchaí*, the traditional Irish keeper of lore and genealogy. Not a
> mythological figure but a real and continuous profession, which is why he is the right narrator
> for the rest of them.

### The Cairn-Keeper
He has stacked a stone for everyone who did not come back and he stopped counting a long time ago.
He stays for the ones still walking. He will not thank you for descending and he does not expect
thanks for waiting.

### Fionnuala the Wandering Fili
A poet who has lost the end of her poem somewhere below and keeps going down after it. She recites
praise-songs to stonework that no longer answers.

> **Of the source —** Fionnuala is the eldest of the **Children of Lir**, turned to a swan by her
> stepmother for nine hundred years. In the source she is a singer of laments rather than a *fili*
> — a trained professional poet — so the promotion is this telling's. It sits well: the Children of
> Lir keep their human voices and their singing is the whole point of the story.

### Tuan mac Cairill
He came over with Partholón's people and outlived them all, and then outlived his own shape several
times over — stag, boar, eagle, salmon — until the grief fit whatever body he was wearing. He
remembers every invasion of Ireland because he was standing in each one. He will remember how
Bres's bridge ends, too. He always does.

> **Of the source —** exactly as the tradition has it, down to the sequence of animal forms and the
> ending — eaten as a salmon by a queen, reborn as her son, and living on to narrate the invasions
> to Saint Finnian. One of the most faithful figures in the deep.

### Ogma Grianainech
Sun-faced Ogma, who cut the first ogham into birch and stood it up as a warning. He will correct
your grip without being asked. At Mag Tuired he took the sword Orna out of Tethra's hand, and when
it was drawn it recited every deed it had ever done — a boastful thing, he says, but it had earned
the boasting.

> **Of the source —** champion of the Tuatha Dé, credited with inventing ogham, and the taking of
> the talking sword Orna from the Fomorian Tethra is in *Cath Maige Tuired*. His epithet
> *grianainech* means "of the sunny countenance"; the wry explanation he gives here — that it is
> the strain of hauling Fomorian dead off the field — is this telling's joke, not the tradition's.

### Clíodhna of the Fair Hair
Queen of the banshees of Munster. She knows the sound of every death that is coming and she has not
heard yours, and she would like you to keep it that way. A wave took her once at Glandore and the
tide still carries her name. She had three bright birds that ate the apples of the Otherworld tree
and sang the sick to sleep and healed them. There are no birds down here.

> **Of the source —** accurate throughout: the Munster otherworld queen, *Tonn Chlíodhna* at
> Glandore, and the three magical birds. Whether she is a banshee-queen or a goddess of love
> depends on which century of the tradition you ask.

### Aoife, Oath-Bound
She carries a grievance with a name attached to it and she will lend it to you. Kill the thing she
names, on the floor she names, and the Otherworld settles something on you for it.

> **Of the source —** *ambiguous, and worth resolving.* The Mythological-Cycle Aoife is the
> stepmother in the **Children of Lir**, who turned Lir's children into swans out of jealousy and
> was made a demon of the air for it — an oath-breaker, not an oath-keeper. The other famous Aoife
> is a warrior queen of the **Ulster Cycle**, out of scope for this deep by your own rule. As
> written she commits to neither. If you want her firmly in-cycle, tying her to the Children of Lir
> — a woman working off an unforgivable thing by proxy — would do it, and would rhyme with
> Fionnuala already wandering the same floors.

### A Fomorian Deserter
He has walked away from the bridge and he wants you to know it can be walked away from. He has seen
what waits on the far side of the causeway and you have not. He will tell you to turn back. He does
not expect it to work.

### A Fomorian Tinker
Sells to anyone. Has no politics beyond that.

> **Of the source —** both invented. The deserter is the more interesting of the two, and he is
> doing real work: he is the evidence for the Seanchaí's claim that the Fomorians were neighbours
> before Bres taught them to take.

## V. Those Taken

Bres's people have been dragging the Tuatha Dé down here under guard, and the falling stone
sometimes brings one past you with their captors still around them. Kill every captor — they will
not move while one still breathes — and they will go up to the mounds and wait for you there. Each
of them has something they are good at, and every one of them is good at it in the way only the
person who invented it can be.

### Gobán Saor, the Wright
Built halls for kings, and Bres's people bound him with stone, which he takes personally. He looks
at the rift's falling rubble with a builder's flat contempt: *Bres sends rubble. Tell me what you
need and I'll send you something with a shape to it.* Name a shape, and the next stone to fall is
that shape.

> **Of the source —** the Gobán Saor is the legendary master builder of **later Irish folklore** —
> the wright of a hundred local stories about clever churches and cleverer trickery — rather than a
> figure of the Mythological Cycle. He is often conflated with Goibniu the smith, whose name he
> half-shares, and who is already downstairs forging your spear. The overlap is the tradition's
> fault, not this telling's, but it is worth knowing the two are not the same hand.

### Bé Chuille the Enchantress
Sits still among her captors with her eyes shut, turning a pebble over. *I asked the stones who was
coming. They said you. Get on with it.* Freed, she reads the floors below: what boss waits, how many
smiths are still unfound. A pebble spins on her palm and does not stop, and where it points the
dark gets thinner.

> **Of the source —** at Mag Tuired the sorceresses **Bé Chuille and Dianann** pledged to enchant
> the trees, the stones and the sods of the earth into an armed host for Lugh. Her line here quotes
> that pledge almost directly. A daughter of Flidais in the genealogies.

### Nuada Airgetlám, of the Silver Hand
Stands bound and unbowed with one hand catching the light wrongly. *They took my kingdom with this
arm. They will not take the arm.* He lost the hand at Mag Tuired, Dian Cécht made him a silver one,
and a blemished man cannot be king — which is the whole reason Bres ever sat the throne. Freed, he
keeps a table in the mound and pours the ale of Goibniu's feast, and he does it the way a king is
supposed to, which is the entire point of the gesture.

> **Of the source —** the arm, the silver replacement, the disqualification and Bres's consequent
> kingship are all as the tradition has them. Calling him a **cook** is a stretch — Nuada is a king
> and a war-leader, not a victualler. But his grievance against Bres is *specifically* about a
> king's table, so a Nuada who feeds people properly is arguing with Bres in the most pointed
> language available to him. A deviation that earns itself.

### Airmed the Herb-Wise
In healer's yellow, ringed by captors who want her to mend their king's ruined flesh. *Cut them
down and I'll tend to worthier wounds.* Three hundred and sixty-five herbs grew on her brother's
grave, one for every joint and sinew in him, and she remembers every one her father scattered. She
does not sell anything. Bring her the herbs you find in the deep — they are vanishingly rare, one
for each of the three hundred and sixty-five — and she works each one into you permanently.

> **Of the source —** Airmed and her brother **Miach** healed Nuada's arm with flesh, outdoing
> their father Dian Cécht, who killed Miach for it. From Miach's grave grew 365 herbs, which Airmed
> laid out on her cloak in their proper order — and Dian Cécht scattered the cloak so the knowledge
> would be lost. The scattering is the wound the whole quest is built on, and reassembling the
> herbs one at a time is exactly the right shape for it.

### Eithne, Balor's Daughter
Bound, entirely unafraid, watching the door rather than her captors. *My own father shut me in a
tower of glass to keep a prophecy from happening. These are an improvement.* A seer told Balor his
grandson would kill him, and Balor was right to be afraid, and it changed nothing. She will look at
the geis you carry and tell you which way it bends — and trade it for another, because a geis can
be traded but never broken.

> **Of the source —** Ethniu, shut in a tower on Tory Island by Balor of the Baleful Eye to prevent
> the prophecy; she bore Lugh regardless, and Lugh killed Balor at Mag Tuired. Faithful. Her trade
> in fates is invented, but a woman whose entire story is *the prophecy happens anyway* is the
> obvious person to run it.

### Dian Cécht, the Physician
An old man in a leech's robe, looking at his captors with professional contempt. *I have put arms
back on kings.* At Mag Tuired he sang over the Well of Sláine with his children and the mortally
wounded walked out of it whole; he can sing a lesser version over you. He mentions, without being
asked, that if his daughter is in the mound they need not speak of her brother.

> **Of the source —** the Well of Sláine and the four-part incantation are in *Cath Maige Tuired*.
> **The deviation is deliberate and load-bearing:** in the source he murders Miach out of
> professional jealousy, and Airmed is his daughter. Putting the two of them in the same room,
> alive and civil, is a softening — but the telling knows it, and that single line about not
> speaking of her brother does more work than a paragraph would. Note the well: **Sláine** is the
> healing well, distinct from the **Well of Segais**, the well of wisdom with its nine hazels and
> its salmon, which is also in the mound. Two different wells, correctly kept apart.

### Midir of Brí Léith
A lord of the sídhe, sitting cross-legged among his captors, calmly setting out a fidchell board.
*They have not worked out that I am waiting for someone. You are late.* He won a queen off a High
King at that board once and was never forgiven for it. In the mound he will play you for gold, as
often as you like — and he wagers honestly, which he notes is more than his last opponent managed.

> **Of the source —** Midir of Brí Léith won **Étaín** from the High King **Eochaid Airem** in a
> series of fidchell games in *Tochmarc Étaíne*, having deliberately lost the early rounds to set
> the stake. His jab at Eochaid's sportsmanship is in character. The board game itself gets its own
> document.

### Abcán the Harper
A small man gagged with iron chain, because they gag the poet first, always. Freed, he hums one low
note and the nearest stones lean in to listen. He plays the **suantraí** — the sleep-strain — down
the stair ahead of you, and whatever wakes down there wakes slowly.

> **Of the source —** Abcán is the dwarf poet-harper of the Tuatha Dé. The **three strains** —
> *goltraí* the lament, *geantraí* the laughter, *suantraí* the sleep-strain — belong most famously
> to **Uaithne, the Dagda's harp**, recovered from the Fomorians after Mag Tuired. Giving the
> sleep-strain to Abcán is a transfer between two harpers of the same people; defensible, and worth
> knowing you have made it.

## VI. The Three Craftsmen, and the Spear

Three of the Tuatha Dé are still working down here, one to a forge, and they are not prisoners.
They are waiting for someone to bring them the rest of the job.

- **Luchta the Carpenter** shapes the **shaft**. Wood shavings curl off his plane, untouched by the
  dark. *A shaft without a wielder is just kindling.*
- **Credne the Brazier** sets the **bolts**. Every rivet and fitting in the old world passed
  through that fire first. *These held Lugh's spear together once. They'll do it again, if you
  carry them true.*
- **Goibniu the Smith** forges the **head**, and the head, he says, is the easy part. Bring him what
  the other two have already given you and he will finish what the old gods started.

Carry all three to Goibniu and the forge roars once, and the **Spear of Lugh** is whole again, and
it answers to you.

> **Of the source —** Goibniu, Luchta and Credne are the *trí dé dána*, the three craft-gods, and
> the division of labour is exactly the tradition's: in *Cath Maige Tuired* they turn out spears
> between them at impossible speed — Goibniu the head in three blows, Luchta the shaft, Credne the
> rivets — and the weapons never miss and the wounds never heal. **Credne is male in every source;**
> this telling makes her a woman, silently. That is a free choice, but it is a choice, and it is the
> only one of the three that has been made.
>
> The **Spear of Lugh** is one of the Four Treasures of the Tuatha Dé, brought from the city of
> Gorias — no battle was ever sustained against it. It is not broken into three parts anywhere in
> the tradition. Reforging it out of pieces is invented, and it is the neatest invention in the
> game: it takes three gods who are *described as a production line* and gives them a production
> line to run.

## VII. The Powers One May Swear To

Some who go down are not fighters, and what they have instead is a deity willing to take their
call. A pact is sworn once and it is not renegotiated. Every one of them takes something.

### The Morrígan
Battle-sorcery — blood-fog, raining fire, and the shriek that breaks a man before a blade reaches
him. Her fury is a loan taken out against your own: everything she grants dulls the arm that holds
the weapon.

> **Of the source —** the battle-goddess, appearing as crow and as prophetess, sometimes one figure
> and sometimes three with **Badb** and **Macha**. *Badb's Shriek* draws on Badb specifically — the
> aspect that terrifies armies off the field rather than killing them on it, which is precisely
> what the spell does.

### Manannán mac Lir
Glamour and sea-mist. The **Féth Fíada** hides the sworn from every mortal eye. The tides quicken to
answer him, and the stone falls faster for it.

> **Of the source —** the sea-god, ruler of Emain Ablach, keeper of the mist. The *féth fíada* is
> the magical concealing mist of the Tuatha Dé, associated with him and with their withdrawal into
> the mounds — the very thing that put the sídhe underground in the first place. Fitting for a
> deity you meet under one.

### Tethra
Parasitic sorcery out of the netherworld. Pay in blood and take back more than you gave. He feeds
as he gives, and every spell tithes your own vitality.

> **Of the source —** and this one is a knife in the dark: **Tethra is a Fomorian king.** His sword
> Orna is the one Ogma takes at Mag Tuired — Ogma, who is upstairs teaching you your grip. He rules
> in Mag Mell over the dead. Swearing to Tethra means taking Bres's side in the middle of Bres's
> war, and nothing in the game says so out loud. Whether that silence is a gap or the best-kept
> secret in the deep is your call; either way it is the sharpest bit of characterisation available
> here and it is currently free money on the table.

## VIII. The Geasa

Power taken in the deep binds itself to you as a **geis**. They come in three weights: the small
ones you find often, the greater ones that come of altars and hard fights, and the old ones that
are not really given so much as permitted.

**The lesser geasa** — *the workaday bindings.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Whetstone** | The edge that does not dull. Every blow lands heavier. |
| **Vital Crystal** | More life in the body than the body was built for. |
| **Iron Scale** | Every strike arrives blunted, always, whoever throws it. |
| **Iron Ward** | Poison finds nothing in you to work on. |
| **Mending Drip** | The body closes itself, slowly, without being asked. |
| **Purifying Spring** | What is inflicted on you burns off faster than it should. |
| **Sight Shard** | The dark stands further off. |
| **Gravity Well** | The falling stone falls slower. Time, bought by the handful. |

**The greater geasa** — *taken from altars and from things that did not want to give them.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Bloodtap Core** | Every death feeds you. |
| **Cruelty Core** | Killing sharpens the arm, and the sharpening lasts as long as the floor does. |
| **Ghost Step** | Blows pass through where you were. |
| **Mist Cloak** | And every blow that misses you closes a wound. |
| **Thornweave Core** | Whoever strikes you is struck back, out of the same wound. |
| **Riftblast Core** | A cleared line comes down on everything alive in the room. |
| **Void Loop** | Every so-many-th blow lands perfectly, without regard for luck. |

**The old geasa** — *these are not granted. They are permitted.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Deathward Rune** | The mounds owe you a death, and will pay it once a floor. |
| **Banshee's Keening** | Nothing near you can act. They stand and listen instead. |
| **Annihilation Rune** | A cleared line kills the room, and kills it harder the deeper you are. |
| **Rift Tide** | The stone slows and the knowing quickens. |
| **Void Prism** | Every other binding you carry makes this one stronger. |

> **Of the source —** a **geis** in the Irish tradition is a *prohibition*, not a gift: a taboo laid
> on a hero which brings ruin when broken. Cú Chulainn is destroyed by two of his geasa
> contradicting each other; Diarmuid is bound by one; Conaire Mór's downfall in *Togail Bruidne Dá
> Derga* is a long, beautiful cascade of geasa violated one after another. **Using the word for a
> beneficial power inverts it completely.** That may well be a deliberate reframing — the deep gives
> you power and the power binds you — but nothing in the game currently says so, and the inversion
> is invisible as a result. One line from the Seanchaí about what a geis costs would convert an
> apparent error into the best piece of theming in the game. See the appendix.

## IX. The Ogham Marks

The tattooist's work. A mark is cut into you and stays cut; five in a life is the most any body will
hold. Marks of a kind call to each other — gather enough of one and the set wakes into something far
past the sum of its parts.

| Mark | Alone | Complete |
|---|---|---|
| **War** | The arm strikes harder. | The arm strikes twice as hard. |
| **Forge** | A cleared line bites deeper. | It bites vastly deeper. |
| **Sick** | What you strike sometimes sickens. | What you strike always sickens. |
| **Cryo** | The falling stone hesitates. | It hesitates longer — and your blows freeze what they touch. |
| **Life** | More life in the body. | One death undone — at the cost of every mark you carry, all of them, at once. |
| **Guard** | Blows arrive blunted. | Blunter still. |
| **Leech** | Every kill knits you back together. | Considerably more of you comes back. |
| **Ghost** | Blows sometimes pass through you. | Often — and once a floor, certainly. |
| **Sight** | The dark stands off. | The dark stands well off. |
| **Speed** | *(nothing, alone)* | You move twice for every once the deep moves. |

Note the Life mark's price. It does not take hit points or gold. It takes the character you spent
the whole descent assembling, and hands you back a living body with nothing written on it.

> **Of the source —** **ogham** is a real Irish script of scored lines along an edge, cut on
> standing stones from roughly the fourth century, and Ogma is credited with its invention — he is
> upstairs, and he says so himself. The idea of ogham as marks cut into a *person* is invented; in
> the tradition it goes on stone and wood. The names, though, are the problem. See the appendix.

## X. Small Goods

What the Fear Dearg keeps on his stall, and the one thing that cannot be bought.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Hearth Broth** | Whatever is in it, you stand up afterwards. |
| **Bogwood Charm** | Black oak out of the bog, older than the bog. There is more of you while you carry it. |
| **Ogham-Etched Edge** | Someone has cut the right letters along the blade. It bites accordingly. |
| **Deathward Sigil** | Buys back one death, this floor only. The mounds do not extend credit past the stair. |
| **A herb of Miach's grave** | Not for sale, and no more than a handful will ever be found. Three hundred and sixty-five grew on that grave and her father scattered every one. Bring them to Airmed. |

> **Of the source —** the **Fear Dearg** ("red man") is a solitary fairy of Irish folklore, a
> practical joker in red, and a merchant of exactly his reputation. **Bogwood** is genuine — oak
> preserved black in peat for millennia, prized and carved. Both are folkloric rather than
> Mythological-Cycle, which is right for a stallholder; gods do not run stalls.

## Appendix — Where the reckoning runs thin

Two places, and both are naming rather than substance. Everything above this line is on-brand; these
two are the exceptions, and both are cheap to fix.

**1. Half the Geasa are not Irish at all.** *Banshee's Keening* and *Deathward Rune* carry the
setting. *Whetstone*, *Sight Shard*, *Iron Scale* and *Gravity Well* are neutral enough to pass. But
**Void Loop, Void Prism, Riftblast Core, Thornweave Core, Bloodtap Core, Cruelty Core** and
**Annihilation Rune** read as science fiction, and the word *Core* appears five times. They are the
only place in the game where the fiction drops. Renaming is free — none of them has mechanical
identity tied to its name — and the tradition is not short of candidates: the Dagda's cauldron that
left no one unsatisfied, Goibniu's ale, the Salmon of Knowledge, Manannán's crane bag, the corrguinecht
posture, the Cauldron of Rebirth.

Related, and worth deciding on deliberately: **a geis is a prohibition, not a boon.** Inverting it
without comment reads as a mistake. One Seanchaí line — *"they call them gifts. A geis was never a
gift. It is a thing you may no longer do, and the deep is generous with them"* — turns the same
mechanic into the best-themed thing in the game at the cost of a single string.

**2. The Ogham Marks are not named for ogham.** *War*, *Life*, *Guard*, *Sight* and *Speed* are
English common nouns; *Cryo*, *Sick* and *Leech* are game-design vocabulary. The script has twenty
letters, each already carrying a tree and a body of association — **Beith, Luis, Fearn, Sail, Nion,
Uath, Dair, Tinne, Coll, Ceirt, Muin, Gort, nGéadal, Straif, Ruis, Ailm, Onn, Úr, Eadhadh, Iodhadh**
— and you need exactly ten. *Tinne* (holly, metal, the smith's letter) for War. *Dair* (oak) for
Guard. *Straif* (blackthorn, and the root of the word for sulphur) for Sick. *Iodhadh* (yew, the
churchyard tree, death and rebirth) for Life. The mapping practically writes itself, and it would
turn a list of stat words into the one system in the game that teaches the player something real
about Ireland.
